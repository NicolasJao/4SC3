The two fundamental diagrams show that 100% AV traffic is faster but breaks down earlier than 100% HDV traffic. The AV scenario has a free-flow speed of 16.57 m/s, roughly double the HDV value of 8.20 m/s, which suggests that AVs travel consistently at the limit while human drivers are held back by more variable behaviour. In contrast, the AV jam density is much lower (about 137 veh/km versus 357 veh/km), so its critical point sits at a lower density (about 69 versus 178 veh/km) but a higher speed (about 8.3 versus 4.1 m/s). This means AV traffic transitions into congestion at a lower density, and its speed falls much more steeply as density increases, with a fitted slope of about 0.12 m/s per veh/km compared with about 0.023 for HDVs. The AV data form two clear clusters, free-flowing at roughly 40 to 48 veh/km and queued at roughly 115 to 135 veh/km, so that fit is well constrained and shows a sharp shift between flowing and jammed states. The HDV data are concentrated in a narrow band of about 60 to 90 veh/km, so its free-flow speed and especially its jam density of 357 veh/km are long extrapolations and should be treated with caution. Overall, AVs give higher speeds at low to moderate densities but a sharper and earlier collapse into congestion, while HDV traffic degrades more gradually over the limited range observed. The reason the AV traffic breaks down here is likely due to the high number of vehicles on the road.
